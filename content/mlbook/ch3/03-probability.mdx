Probability theory provides the mathematical framework for quantifying uncertainty. It allows us to assign a numerical value — between 0 and 1 — to our belief that a particular event will occur.

## Sample Space and Events

The **sample space** $\Omega$ is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment. An **event** $A$ is a subset of the sample space: $A \subseteq \Omega$.

For example, when rolling a six-sided die:

$$\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$$

The event "rolling an even number" is $A = \{2, 4, 6\}$.

## Axioms of Probability

Kolmogorov's axioms define the rules that any probability measure $P$ must satisfy:

1. **Non-negativity**: $P(A) \geq 0$ for every event $A$
2. **Normalization**: $P(\Omega) = 1$
3. **Additivity**: For mutually exclusive events $A$ and $B$:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

## Conditional Probability

The probability of event $A$ given that event $B$ has occurred is defined as:

$$P(A | B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad P(B) > 0$$

This is one of the most important concepts in machine learning. When we train a classifier, we are essentially learning $P(Y | X)$ — the probability of a label $Y$ given features $X$.

## Independence

Two events $A$ and $B$ are **independent** if knowing that one occurred does not change the probability of the other:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$$

Equivalently, $P(A | B) = P(A)$.

In practice, the assumption of independence is powerful but often unrealistic. The **Naive Bayes** classifier (Chapter 9) makes this assumption explicit — and despite its "naive" nature, it performs remarkably well in many real-world scenarios.
