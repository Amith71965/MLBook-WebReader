Bayes' rule is arguably the single most important equation in machine learning. It provides a principled way to update our beliefs about the world as we observe new evidence.

## The Formula

Given two events $A$ and $B$, Bayes' rule states:

$$P(A | B) = \frac{P(B | A) \cdot P(A)}{P(B)}$$

Each term has a specific name and interpretation:

| Term | Name | Interpretation |
|------|------|---------------|
| $P(A \| B)$ | **Posterior** | Our updated belief about $A$ after observing $B$ |
| $P(B \| A)$ | **Likelihood** | How probable the evidence $B$ is, given $A$ |
| $P(A)$ | **Prior** | Our initial belief about $A$ before seeing evidence |
| $P(B)$ | **Evidence** | The total probability of observing $B$ |

## A Practical Example

Suppose a medical test for a rare disease has the following characteristics:

- The disease affects 1% of the population: $P(D) = 0.01$
- The test correctly identifies sick patients 99% of the time: $P(+ | D) = 0.99$
- The test incorrectly flags healthy patients 5% of the time: $P(+ | \neg D) = 0.05$

**Question**: If a patient tests positive, what is the probability they actually have the disease?

Using Bayes' rule:

$$P(D | +) = \frac{P(+ | D) \cdot P(D)}{P(+)}$$

First, we compute $P(+)$ using the law of total probability:

$$P(+) = P(+ | D) \cdot P(D) + P(+ | \neg D) \cdot P(\neg D)$$

$$P(+) = 0.99 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99 = 0.0099 + 0.0495 = 0.0594$$

Therefore:

$$P(D | +) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.01}{0.0594} \approx 0.167$$

This surprising result — only a 16.7% chance of actually having the disease despite a positive test — illustrates the **base rate fallacy**. When a disease is rare (low prior), even a highly accurate test produces many false positives.

## Bayesian Inference in ML

In machine learning, Bayes' rule takes the form:

$$P(\theta | \mathcal{D}) = \frac{P(\mathcal{D} | \theta) \cdot P(\theta)}{P(\mathcal{D})}$$

where $\theta$ represents model parameters and $\mathcal{D}$ is the observed data. This is the foundation of **Bayesian learning**, where we maintain a distribution over possible parameter values rather than a single point estimate.

> The Bayesian perspective stands in contrast to the **frequentist** approach, where parameters are treated as fixed (but unknown) values. Both perspectives have their merits, and modern machine learning draws from both traditions.
